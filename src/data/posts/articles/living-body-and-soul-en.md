## How Should Chinese Homes Be Built to Shelter the Body and Settle the Mind?

So many times I've stood at the window of a rented apartment, looking at the residential buildings rising in front of me, watching the silent pantomime of family life playing out behind each lit window — some warm, some lonely, some hung with exaggerated red lanterns flickering in five colors, others holding nothing but an empty sofa and a TV.

And what I find myself wondering is: when I finally have my own place, can it please not be blocked, so I can see the scenery outside?

The Chinese feel about renovation is as complicated as an old pot of simmering broth: it holds parents' expectations, children's futures, neighbors' judgments, the "premium feel" of short-video feeds, and the无处安放 dignity of whatever is left in the bank account.

Some say renovation is the last great "dream" of adulthood. Before a single wall is built, we've already lived the ideal life in our heads: sipping coffee by the floor-to-ceiling window at dawn, laughing over the kitchen island at night, reading Zhuangzi on the balcony on weekends.

But the reality is almost always this: no time to get up at dawn, TikTok unfinished at night, and on weekends we just want hotpot.

So — is renovation for "sheltering the body," or for "settling the mind"?

---

### I. "Sheltering the Body": China's Model-Home Worship

The biggest problem with Chinese renovation is that we constantly mistake "what others think is good" for "what makes us live well."

We only need a comfortable home, yet we insist on building a hybrid of a five-star hotel, a Nordic B&B, and an Italian concept store.

This is "model-home worship."

The bigger the marble, the more premium it feels; the more light strips, the more atmosphere; the more elaborate the TV wall, the more status it signals.

Behind this lies a deep cultural psychology of seeking approval from the outside — a sign of an inwardly "weak" culture.

True sheltering of the body is not about making the house look like a magazine cover, but about letting life flow smoothly. The elderly don't trip getting up at midnight; children can grow and be themselves; cooking, laundry, and rest flow naturally into one another. A home is first and foremost "the body's infrastructure," not a "photography studio for the friend circle."

The first principle of renovation should not be "premium feel," but "1234" — one room, two people, three meals, four seasons.

The humid return of spring, takeout boxes in summer, seasonal clothes in autumn, heating gods in winter. To keep your dignity through all that reality — that is a true design master.

---

### II. Revisiting "Settling the Mind": Young People's Empty Rooms, Tired Hearts?

Today many young people have realized the problem of "over-renovation," and so they've gone the other way: minimalism, raw wood, blank space, wabi-sabi.

The home is empty and large — yet still can't hold my exhaustion.

"Settling the mind" is not simply about decluttering; it is about creating an order that can house your moods.

Traditional Chinese dwellings actually understood this well. The ancients spoke of "a bright hall, a quiet bedroom, an elegant study, a leisurely courtyard" — space itself carries an emotional function.

A tree in the courtyard, a small table by the window, a scroll of calligraphy on the wall — none of these were for "the photo," but to give people a moment of calm in daily life.

A desk lamp one willingly switches on at night; a chair the father uses specially for tea; a wall plastered with travel tickets and the children's doodles.

A home becomes a home not because it is "perfect," but because it has "warmth."

The biggest regret for many during renovation is throwing away everything old. The old wooden chest, the worn bookcase, the sewing machine left by grandmother — seemingly "not modern enough," yet they can make one feel "this is my life" far more than any imported ornament.

---

### III. The Two-Generation Renovation War: Parents Want "Face," Youth Want "Ease"

The Chinese renovation site is often also a set for family-ethics drama.

Parents walk in: "How can the living room have no main light?"
Youth: "No main light creates atmosphere."
Parents: "The TV wall is too plain."
Youth: "Blank space is an aesthetic."
Parents: "Tens of thousands spent and not even a crystal chandelier — what will the relatives think?"
Youth: "I'm not running a wedding banquet hall."

This conflict is essentially a collision of two eras' experiences.

The older generation lived through housing shortages; a home was not just shelter but a symbol of struggle and social status, so they stress "face," "looking proper," "not losing to others."

The younger generation grew up under high housing prices and high pressure, and crave "ease" more. They want home to shed social roles, not keep performing on the stage of "successful person."

Actually neither side is wrong. What's wrong is turning "home" into a battlefield of values.

Home is not a debate — there's no need to decide "who is more advanced."

---

### IV. Can Tradition and Modernity Reconcile?

Mention "New Chinese style" and many immediately picture rosewood furniture, carved screens, and an atmosphere "as if a family ancestor-worship ritual might begin any second."

But what is truly valuable in traditional culture is not the symbols, but the wisdom.

For instance: "blank space" reminds us not to cram the room full; "borrowed scenery" tells us a tree outside the window has more life than ten ornaments indoors; "facing south" reflects respect for natural light and ventilation; "a family gathered around the table" emphasizes connection, not the table's price.

Modern life has its own reason too: the dishwasher frees our hands, smart lighting adds convenience, ergonomic furniture protects the spine.

A truly living home is never a "retro replica," but a union of "traditional spirit + modern tools."

You can absolutely place a smart speaker on a sandalwood table, a coffee machine beside Song-style calligraphy, and burn incense in a tatami corner while joining a remote meeting —

and that is precisely the most real and most charming shape of contemporary Chinese life.

---

### V. A Few Suggestions for "Sheltering Body and Mind"

**First, prioritize your budget for the things you touch every day.**
Mattress, sofa, lighting, hardware, kitchen counter — ten times more important than the feature wall.

**Second, keep at least one "functionless corner."**
It need not serve storage, work, or display — only the job of letting you zone out. The most luxurious space for an adult is often the place where you can do absolutely nothing.

**Third, don't "finish" the home all at once.**
A truly good home grows slowly with living. A cup brought back from travel, a painting a friend gave you, a sticker the child suddenly slaps on one day — all gradually give the space a soul.

**Fourth, ask yourself one question regularly:**
"Is this design to make my life easier, or to prove to others that I know how to live?"

---

### Conclusion: The Ultimate Meaning of a House

In the end, "sheltering the body" solves shelter from wind and rain; "settling the mind" solves the settling of the soul.

The former keeps us from being displaced; the latter keeps us from losing our inner softness amid steel and concrete.

The special predicament of this generation of Chinese is this: we have both experienced a fierce hunger for "having a home," and begun to ask "what counts as truly living." Therefore renovation should not be merely a game of materials, styles, and budget, but a self-examination of one's philosophy of life.

The best home need not be the biggest, the priciest, or the trendiest.

When the body can relax, that is "living comfortably"; when the mind can slowly quiet down, that is "living with peace of mind."

This may be the most plain yet most rare answer to Chinese home renovation: able to "shelter the body," and more so to "settle the mind" — so that a house is not merely an asset, but a true human world.
