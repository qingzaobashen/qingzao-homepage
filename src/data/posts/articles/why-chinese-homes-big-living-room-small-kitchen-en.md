# Why Do Chinese Homes Have Huge Living Rooms but Tiny Kitchens and Bathrooms?

Let me start with a story.

A couple of years ago I visited a friend — a 200-plus-square-meter flat, with a living room big enough to play badminton in. Then I went to the bathroom and my knee hit the wall. Not an exaggeration. It hit the wall. I came out and asked him about it; he said, "Oh, we didn't really pay attention to that one."

Two hundred square meters, and in the end his knee got stuck in two of them.

I also saw his TV wall: an entire slab of sintered stone, lit so that it reflected mountains, rivers and lakes. He said it cost over seventy thousand — seventy-six or seventy-eight thousand, I'm bad with numbers, anyway more than seventy.

I told him the money was well spent. He said, "Right?"

Now, if you explain all this with "Chinese people care about face," you're not wrong — but it's useless, because it doesn't explain the mechanism. The mechanism goes like this.

---

## 1. The living room isn't for guests. It's for the thirty seconds of the viewing

Walk into a sales office and the first piece of paper they hand you is the floor plan. What looks big on that plan? The living room.

It's the only room in the whole apartment that has any presence on a flat drawing — one glance and you can tell "this living room is generous." The bathroom is a small square in the corner. Draw 4 m² and 2.5 m² side by side and your brain registers no difference at all.

Developers know this perfectly well: give generously where it can be perceived, build to the floor where it can't.

And where is the floor? In the national Residential Design Code, the usable kitchen area must be no less than 4 m², and a bathroom holding the full set — toilet, shower, basin — no less than 2.5 m². That 2.5 was itself revised down from 3.0 in the 2011 edition.

Note that: the code itself is moving downwards, and developers simply build to the lower limit, not one square meter more. This isn't negligence. It's precision.

Now think about how buying actually works. You follow the agent through the door, stand in the entrance and look around — thirty seconds, maybe — and you've already decided whether to keep talking. What do you see in those thirty seconds? The living room. You don't push the door open and say, "Sit on the toilet, let me have a look." You don't crouch down to measure whether the kitchen counter runs a full three meters.

Developers understand this better than anyone. So they take the one part of the floor plan they can freely arrange, and pour into it all the money, all the area, and the entire visual budget.

---

## 2. The furniture in the show apartment is fake

This is an open trick in the industry: the bed, the sofa and the dining table in a show apartment are custom-made, one size smaller than the real thing, so the rooms look bigger.

But here's what you should notice — you never step into the show apartment's bathroom to take a shower. It's the only room that is never part of the experience.

Where nobody looks, nobody has to try very hard.

---

## 3. Hotels do the math even harder

I've never built a house, and I'm no expert in hotel operations, so let me be clear upfront: what follows is my own reading, not any industry consensus.

Hotel bathrooms are small not because guests don't matter. Quite the opposite — because the operator has calculated everything down to the last digit.

Guests book a room for the bed, the window, the location. Nobody pays two hundred extra because the bathroom is eight square meters. In other words, the bathroom is pure cost, not a selling point. It's also the most expensive square meter in the building: waterproofing, floor slopes, drains, glass partitions — construction and long-term maintenance both run far higher than laying down a carpet. One extra square meter means one more square meter for the housekeeper to scrub, and room turnaround time creeps up. I'm not certain exactly how many times more expensive it is, but it is clearly, significantly more expensive.

So the hotel logic is: spend the money where you lie down and where your eyes land when you wake up, and deliver everything else at the minimum standard of "not uncomfortable."

**Small is not the problem. Shoddy is.**

I've stayed in Japanese unit bathrooms — so small you can barely turn around — but they had wet-dry separation, thermostatic hot water and proper ventilation, all in place. You feel cramped in there, but you feel dignified. Too many of ours are the opposite: not large either, and they leak, they smell, the silicone sealant turns black. That's what actually makes people angry.

---

## The plainest way to say it

Space has never been allocated by how long you stay in it. It's allocated by whether it can be sold.

A living room can be sold — buyers perceive it, agents can photograph it, relatives walk in and go "wow." A bathroom can't be sold; at the moment you sign the contract, its dimensions don't exist in your head at all.

So the living room isn't built for strangers who visit twice a year. It's built for the five minutes in which the deal closes.
