# From OP to Trash: The September 2026 AI Coding Tool Power Ranking

Let me start with a horror story: that "deep-dive AI coding tool review" you bookmarked in June 2026? Opening it now is roughly the equivalent of unearthing an artifact.

This past year the industry moved at a pace like this: OpenAI renamed its coding models in one breathless string (5.1, 5.1 Max, 5.2, 5.3, 5.4 — retired just on August 31, 5.5, 5.6, all the way to GPT-6); Anthropic handed naming rights to the literature department — while everyone else ships "3.8 Max," they ship "Fable" and "Mythos" — and "Fable 5" was even pulled from sale once this year before being re-listed on July 1. A single fable, and they somehow produced a director's cut.

So before we rank, let's set the rules: in this industry, even the referees can't be trusted. The old-school software-engineering benchmark (SWE-bench Verified) was officially deprecated by OpenAI this February, on the grounds that 59% of its problems had leaked into everyone's training sets; and from ranks 8 through 15, eight models from seven labs are bunched between 80.6% and 80.0% — a gap of just 0.6 points. To sum it up in one line: when the exam leaks, what's the point of testing? Eight contestants crammed into 0.6 points — this isn't a leaderboard, it's Spring Festival travel season.

So this article ignores the ads and looks at real-world results. You've had your turn, now here's mine. Five tiers:

- **S-Tier (夯)**: the main characters — all the camera time, budget, and controversy live here.
- **A-Tier (顶级)**: the key supporting cast, with their own storylines, occasionally stealing the show.
- **B-Tier (人上人)**: "better than the rest" — the reliable workhorses whose fans spam "honestly, it's pretty good."
- **NPCs (NPC)**: they don't affect the plot — they stand on the street, hand out quests, serve as background.
- **F-Tier (拉)**: the character is dead. Rest in peace.

## S-Tier: The World Revolves Around Them

**Claude Code: the reigning ball king — but the king charges admission.** Topped the tool ranking in September, highest score on the WebDev Arena leaderboard (1762, up 73 from the previous gen); in the developer sentiment survey its "most loved" rate was 46%, versus 19% for second and 9% for third — the love is real, and so is the threshold: no free tier, $20 to $200 a month. Even more real: about 43% of the time, you're debugging the code it wrote. The bugs it produces are more confident than yours.

**Cursor: the feel ceiling — and its boss went to space.** On August 14, SpaceX closed the acquisition of its parent company, and it officially changed surnames. The product itself remains the best daily editing experience; its enterprise spend share did drop from 41% to 26%, but that's "merger pains," not a product problem. Users' mood now is subtle: I just wanted to write some code, and my boss turned out to be someone who wants to go to Mars. At this rate, committing code will be called "launch," and rollback will be "failed landing."

**OpenAI Codex: the one who skipped the exam — and topped the mock.** In February it announced "the exam leaked so we're not taking the real test," then turned around and, on the Terminal-Bench benchmark, GPT-6 edged Anthropic's 57.9% with 58.2% — the only front where the whole company still fights back. The CLI tool is open-source and free, with a clear positioning: dispatch async tasks, collect results. The downside is a family tree messier than *Dream of the Red Chamber* — 5.4 was buried last month, and 6 already ascended the throne.

## A-Tier: The Tier With the Most New Plotlines

Let's start with the open-source heroes — this tier was half contracted out to open source.

OpenCode, with a license permissive enough for commercial use, 75+ models you can plug in, and air-gapped deployment support, tops the code-hosting platform's most-popular-agent list with 198k stars. Poor yet unyielding, it never drops its lofty ambitions.

Cline did something clever: in May it spun its open-source SDK out into infrastructure, and now GitHub and Anthropic are both playing the same move — it got copied so hard it became an industry standard. One black mark too: its CLI installer suffered a supply-chain attack, a reminder to everyone not to treat auto-approve as a religion.

Aider is "the legend everyone's heard of": repo map, atomic commits, multi-language benchmarks — the whole industry copied them, and then its release cadence entered retirement mode.

Goose is even more extreme: while others fear acquisition, it literally joined a monastery — donated to a neutral Agentic AI Foundation, sharing a household registry with the MCP standard.

OpenHands learned to "borrow troops": via the Agent Client Protocol (ACP), it can stuff Claude Code and Codex inside itself as hired labor.

Crush is the公认 (universally acknowledged) looks-ceiling of terminal UIs (TUI) — it's like the terminal got cosmetic surgery; looks are everything.

Three more non-open-source faces. Kiro (AWS): everyone else starts coding the moment you finish describing requirements, but Kiro insists on first generating three documents — requirements, design, tasks — and only starts after you approve. Great for teams that need to be audited; terrible for you demoing tonight. Devin Desktop, formerly Windsurf — this soap opera deserves its own episode: OpenAI tried to buy it for ~$3B (fell through), Google paid $2.4B to poach the CEO and co-founder, Cognition scooped it up for $250M, it was renamed remotely in June, and the old product was buried in July. The product isn't bad, but making my editor change three parents in a year? Nobody signed up for that. Google Antigravity deserves a special mention: the personal tier is $0, unlimited code completion, and you can pick among Gemini, Claude, and gpt-oss — for the first time in history, a free Google tool lets you mooch off Claude. The only catch: given Google's track record of killing products, use it for now, but don't renovate the house around it.

Oh, and Amp: its plans are named "Megawatt" ($20) and "Gigawatt" ($200), four intensity tiers from low to ultra-high — other agents burn credits, this one burns electricity; flip on the top tier and your home meter starts cosplaying a nuclear plant.

## B-Tier: The Biggest Roster This Year, the Worst Oversaturation

First, my "official wife," GitHub Copilot. 1.8M paid users, ~42% market share, $10 a month (the cheapest on the board), switched to credits billing from June — it's the most widespread coding tool on earth, and the least loved: only 9% "most loved" rate. The marriage is real, the passion is not, but when you try to divorce you realize every approval workflow in the company was built around it. Official-wife status, distant-relative treatment — that's B-Tier.

**The domestic (Chinese) squad is the focus this time.** Trae (ByteDance) lets you touch Claude 4.6 on its free tier, and by the end of last year had already accumulated 6M signups and 1.6M monthly actives. But the plot is very ByteDance: on February 24 it changed "600 high-speed requests per month" to usage-based billing, and old users wailed that "the good old days are gone"; from July it started popping up "today's chat quota is used up," conveniently pointing you to a paid priority queue called "Express." The classic three-step: free to attract, quietly tighten, gently pressure to pay. The privacy terms are "telemetry cannot be opted out, data retained 5 years after account deletion" — whether you use it depends on whether your code is worth the ticket price.

Qoder CN (Alibaba Cloud, ex-Tongyi Lingma) took a Gartner Magic Quadrant Challenger seat, ¥99 a seat, and the product is genuinely not bad. But the family drama rivals a Qing palace opera: Lingma was renamed on May 20, the old SKU stopped selling on September 15, renewals end April 2027, and it's fully shut down in September; it also sentenced its own collaboration workbench to death. Renaming, replacing, and funeral — all in one year. Old users' mood: did I buy a tool, or a boat ticket?

CodeBuddy (Tencent Cloud) holds the strongest domestic internal endorsement: covering Tencent's 12,000 engineers, with 90% of its own code generated by AI — literally, I generate myself. In April it launched a cloud agent literally named "NPC," officially explained as "Non-Programmer Colleague" — on this list, "NPC" is a tier; for them it's the proper name. On top of that there's the "crayfish family bucket": four products, one crayfish eaten four ways, shared credits — there's always one that gets you to pay.

Kimi Code, from Moonshot AI, dual-form CLI plus editor extension, defaulting to the K2.7 coding model with a switch to K3, direct domestic access, Alipay/WeChat payment — the most beginner-friendly installment. As Kimi, I'll stop here; say more and you'll close the tab.

Zhipu's ZCode does conversational development via "controlling your PC from your phone" — the most aggressive route; DeepSeek open-sourced its agent framework, shouting "the Android of the agent era." The domestic squad runs five parallel routes, none of them copying Cursor.

**Overseas stragglers**: JetBrains AI, $10 for multi-model built-in, is the dowry for JetBrains users — you may not buy it, but it has to be there. For Devin Cloud, memorize the term "compute unit" (ACU): $20 is just the entry ticket, heavy use can push the bill past $2,000 — its billing method deserves heritage protection. Augment fully abandoned individual users, $100/month for 50 seats, focusing on big-corp business — the individual market, for it, was a love that passed. Kilo Code adopted all of Roo Code's orphans, open-source, 500+ models to pick from — the open-source world's receiver of last resort, and it received them with dignity. v0 is the React-UI ceiling, but goes limp off the Vercel deployment ecosystem. Qodo and CodeRabbit do intelligent code review — a teleprompter, not an actor; they don't take the blame for a good or bad show, but they hand over the lines. Tabby is self-hosted code completion, the romance of the intranet shut-in. Among the rag-tag terminal army (Gemini CLI, DeepSeek TUI, avante.nvim), the first is a 100k+ star open-source blue-blood, except its parent has already named an heir; the latter two are the last stand of the CLI party and the vim party, respectively.

## NPCs: On the Street, But Each With a Role

Tabnine and Cody both did the same thing this year: killed the individual free tier and retreated to enterprise. One is $39–$59 a seat, the other $59 enterprise-only; both sell "code never leaves the intranet." They're not failing — they're deliberately pivoting to character actors: finance, defense, medicine, offline deployment, expensive appearance fees, one line of dialogue: compliance.

The Vibe Coding quartet (Replit, Bolt, Lovable, etc.) deserves a warning: in the "programmer universe" they're NPCs, but in the "entrepreneur-who-can't-code universe" they're the protagonists. But on this professional-developer board, they share the same fatal flaw: **after the demo, the magic vanishes.** Cross-reviews found: Bolt burns through the free quota in a single prompt; Lovable was crowned "the laziest agent," charging for five steps of debugging at five steps' worth of cost; Base44 claims full-stack but imports no npm packages; Replit is slightly better, all-in-one and worry-free, but "one account equals editor equals server equals invoice" — recite that three times before using.

Tongyi Lingma (old SKU): a three-act curtain call, shut down later than Amazon Q — a "chronic" decline. QoderWork CN: online just over a year before shutting down; even the obituary is very Alibaba-flavored: R&D scenarios migrate to the desktop version, office scenarios to Qwen, credits don't transfer, refunds you negotiate with sales yourself. Void: archived in June, idealism fully filed away. Continue: final version shipped, maintenance stopped, official advice "keep using old deployments, but don't make it the default" — translated: hold a vigil, but don't throw a wedding.

The finale is always the referee: software-engineering benchmarks themselves, 59% leaked, eight labs crammed into 0.6 points. When the referee and the players hug it out, what we're watching is Spring Festival travel season.

## The Wrap-Up

84% of developers are already using or planning to use such tools, and on average they use 2.3 at once. So the question "which is best" expired in September 2026 — but this list still wants to tell you one structural fact: of 44 seats, B-Tier holds 17, the biggest roster. The top three eat the meat, the 17 in the middle fight for soup, and the bottom lives on a permanent life-and-death line. That also explains this year's collective pivot: Copilot switched to credits, Cursor got acquired, Trae switched to usage billing, Alibaba is in mourning, Tabnine and Augment abandoned individuals — the middle is collapsing, everyone is running to the two extremes of "enterprise wallets" and "free-to-attract," and the individual paying users caught in the middle are being politely shown the door.

So the advice for individual developers: pick one S-Tier tool as your main, pick one open-source A-Tier as your backup (it's free anyway), then buckle up — at the update frequency of this death list, next month this table will be obsolete again.

The greatest stability in this industry is instability itself.
